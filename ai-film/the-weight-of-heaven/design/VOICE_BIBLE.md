# The Weight of Heaven: voice bible

How the film sounds when people speak, and when they do not. Built on the third draft and the Visual Bible. Everything here is a proposal until the author approves it; once approved, each character's **Voice DNA** paragraph is copied unchanged into every auteur video prompt in which they speak (the template's VOICE DNA block), and into every voice-generation brief.

The line book, every spoken line with its scene, speaker and delivery, is `audio/lines.json` (45 lines, 8 speakers; 20 of them carry the film and get three takes).

---

## 1. Principles

1. **The picture says it before the line does.** The film has 45 lines in 14 minutes. Voices never explain what the frame shows; they arrive after it.
2. **Weight is in the voice too.** Above, voices are unhurried and breath-light: nobody has ever had to push. Below, voices sit low in the chest and carry the breath of effort. On the ground, Serath's own breath gets heavier (6.2 onward), and nothing else about her voice changes.
3. **Two registers, not two races.** The cast is mixed on both sides. What a voice sounds like comes from where the person lives and what they carry, never from their face.
   - **The high speech** (Orison: court, cathedral, fleet) is one shared register. It is a soft, precise, unhurried English close to Received Pronunciation, but older and gentler than a modern one: long vowels, consonants placed lightly, no regional marks, no military bark.
   - **The low speech** (the ground) is regional and plain, and each nation has its own colour. Grammar is slightly blunter, words are fewer, and the voice sits close to the chest.
   - **Karst is between the two.** He has the grammar of the court with the vowels of the ground.
4. **Nobody shouts.** Not the Hierophant in a cathedral of ten thousand, not Marl at the wheel, not the Flag Captain giving the order to let go all anchors. The loudest sound a person makes in this film is the boy's single laugh (11.3).
5. **Silence is a line.** "Nobody speaks. There is nothing yet that can be said." Scene 11 has no dialogue. Scene 10 has two lines in about three minutes. Leave the gaps the script leaves.
6. **Read the line as written.** No added words, no ad-libs, no "Admiral" that is not in the script. The em-dashes are interruptions: the next voice cuts in, or the speaker stops.

## 3. Voice DNA (immutable)

**SERATH (C01).** The high speech of Orison: a soft, unplaced Received Pronunciation, older and gentler than a modern one, with no regional mark. A woman of fifty-five with a low alto, warm but closed, a little grain at the bottom of the range. She speaks at a measured pace and always finishes her sentences; she never hurries and never raises her voice. Above, her breath is silent. On the ground, from the moment she steps off the cutter, her breath is heavier, though her voice itself does not change. Emotion never shows in her pitch. It shows in how long she waits before she answers, and in her sentences getting shorter. Her strongest word in the film, "Anchors," is low, level and unforced.

**KARST (C02).** The grammar of the court spoken with the vowels of the ground: an educated Received Pronunciation flattened toward a northern English plainness by nineteen years below. A man of fifty-six with a dry, tired baritone, a slight rasp, low energy. He is unhurried because he has already done the sums. His sentences end flat and falling, and his humour is so dry it sounds like plain fact. His breath is audible between thoughts. He is never angry. When something matters most he gets quieter, not louder; his most important word, "Slowly," is almost to himself.

**MARL (C03).** The low speech of Anvil Vale: a broad northern English hill-country accent with short vowels and hard consonants, in the Cumbrian and North Yorkshire family. A woman of thirty-four with a mid-low voice made hoarse by dust and two days of digging. She speaks in short sentences, every word set down like a stone, with heavy breath behind it. Her grief is held behind a hard, flat delivery. It leaks once, as a catch in the throat that she cuts off before it becomes anything.

**THE HIEROPHANT (C05).** The high speech at its purest: a liturgical Received Pronunciation, old-fashioned and round. A man of seventy-four with a rich, resonant bass-baritone, deep and full in the breath, a voice used to filling vast rooms without effort. Every sentence has a liturgical cadence and comes to rest on a falling end, like a pronouncement. He is perfectly steady and has never been in doubt. He speaks hard things gently, as a kindness: "Heaven has done your arithmetic for you" is said almost warmly.

**THE CHANCELLOR (C06).** The high speech of Orison, softly. A woman of sixty-four with a tired mezzo-alto, a little husky, slow and plain. She is honest and says hard things without ornament. A slight tremor shows on "grandchildren", then her voice steadies, and "Break it" is quiet and final.

**THE FLAG CAPTAIN (C07).** The high speech as the fleet uses it: crisp, clear, every consonant placed, an officer's reporting voice. A man of forty-five with a mid baritone. He reports facts in order, at an even pace. Under the evenness, the strain rises from line to line. It shows in a gap before "They are carrying beds," and in reading orders aloud that he does not want to read. "I know what it is called" is quiet and resolved; "Let go all anchors" is a clear order, not a shout.

**THE ENGINEER, as sent in Q04 (proposed 26 November, for the author to confirm).** The paragraph below asks for false starts and a cleared throat, which a video model plays as extra words. For Q04 it was rewritten as sound only, and his hesitation is played in the picture: "The high speech of Orison, older and technical: English, a soft Received Pronunciation. A man of seventy-two with a thin, reedy tenor, dry and quiet. He speaks slowly, one short sentence to a breath, exact with numbers, and apologetic."

**THE ENGINEER OF ORISON (C08).** The high speech, older and technical, a little out of practice at court. A man of seventy-two with a thin, reedy tenor. He is hesitant: he starts sentences and stops, clears his throat, and is exact with numbers and vague with consequences. He is apologetic and would rather not say it. His longest pause in the film comes before "Sarn Vale."

**THE TEN THOUSAND (K01).** A vast congregation answering in unison in a cathedral open to the cloud: men, women and children of every age. The response is murmured, not shouted, in an unhurried liturgical cadence. It sounds like habit, not passion, and the nave carries it into a long tail. It is the high speech in thousands of mouths at once.

## 5. Sounds people make that are not lines

- **Applause (3.1):** ten thousand gloved hands, almost soundless, like a soft rain.
- **The aide falls (6.2):** a sharp breath, then a body on dust. No cry.
- **Serath on the ground (6.2 onward):** her breath, heavier.
- **The fighters at the wheel (8.7, 9.11):** effort breaths; Marl's grunt on the spoke.
- **The gunners (10.4):** nothing at all, or one swallowed breath.
- **The boy (11.3):** one laugh that stops short. The only loud human sound in the film.
- **The armies at the landing (10.21):** some of them kneel, with no voices: cloth, knees, armour on stone.
- **The terraces (11.8):** nobody cheers. One thing is thrown and lands short; there is no shout with it.

## 7. How the voices get made (to settle with the author)

- **Voices are designed, never cloned.** No real person's voice or likeness. Each character gets a voice built from its Voice DNA.
- **Tools.** Gemini TTS is connected in this session and can audition voices at once, with the direction passed as metadata so it is never spoken. ElevenLabs (Voice Design and v4, the studio's usual route) needs its key added to the environment. For each character, audition two or three voices against their three hardest lines, rank them by ear and with earshot, and let the author choose. Record each choice in DECISIONS.
- **Takes.** Three takes of every line that carries the film (the table in §4), ranked, then chosen by ear.
- **On camera.** The video models re-voice any audio reference, so dialogue is generated as picture first. Our recorded line is then lip-synced onto it, or the shot is staged so that the mouth is small, far or turned away. Many of the bible's frames already work this way: silhouettes on the bridge, wides on the plain.
- **The ten thousand.** No voice tool can make ten thousand people. Layer several designed voices (men, women and children, eight to twelve takes, each slightly offset in time and pitch) and put them in the cathedral's tail. It must read as a congregation answering, not as chanting.
- **Loudness.** Dialogue sits under the bell, the anchor's note and the hum, never on top of them. The mix rules are in the studio's sound reference.
