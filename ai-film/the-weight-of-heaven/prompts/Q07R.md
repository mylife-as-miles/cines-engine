## The scene, as written

"Below: a plain of black basalt to the horizon, in columns, like a city no one finished. At its edge, across the head of a green valley, stands THE WEIR, the ancient station that takes the weight of Orison. A mile of fused black stone without a seam or a tool-mark. Nine gates. Two are shut." And above: "the underside of the kingdom fills the sky: raw rock, roots, chains thick as towers."

## The shot (one shot, 5 s; about 3 s will be used)

From the air, behind the cutter and a little above it, travelling at its speed. The horizon sits one third of the way down. Across the top, the black underside of Orison running out of the frame; under its rim the band of day; on the horizon the Weir, straight and black, nine tall gates, seven open to the green beyond and two shut; below it the plain, lost in mist far off, with stacks of columns standing out of the mist on either side of a lane that leads to the gates; at the bottom, far beneath us, the column tops streaming past. The cutter is in the lower middle, an eighth of the frame wide, white, flying away from us, a crewman at its tiller.

## What each fault becomes

| What the critics saw | What the new shot does |
| --- | --- |
| Camera too high to show sky; no kingdom in the frame | 45 m up, looking ahead and 8° down: rock, sky, wall and plain in one frame |
| The honeycomb sharp to the horizon: a tabletop | Column tops readable only in the bottom quarter; the far half of the plain is mist |
| The wall a thin line with eight glowing dots; squat here, tall in shot 4 | The wall a twentieth of the frame tall, its gates openings with the valley's green in them; drawn from the same reference as shot 4 |
| The cutter an empty white dinghy on two legs, unlike shot 1's | The cutter of its sheet, seen from behind so that cabin, spire and deck show; a crewman at the tiller; thirty metres up, clear air under it |
| Nothing gives scale | The crewman; gates many times the cutter's size; a far chain as thick as a tower |
| Flat zooms with no parallax | The camera really travels: near stacks pass, the wall holds |

**The "legs" were the cutter's own landing skids.** Its sheet (`refs/SHIP_V04_cutter.jpg`) has two iron skids on struts under the keel and a rudder behind; from high above and behind, on a boat that looked as if it stood on the ground, the critics read them as stilts and a pole, and I painted them out of the edit. The remake keeps them, as designed, and flies the cutter high enough that they read as landing gear.

## What Villeneuve's frames do, and where the shot uses it

Studied from the frames already read this session on StillsLab (Dune: Part One, Dune: Part Two, Blade Runner 2049, Arrival); the site's whole gallery was not fetched.

| Frame | What it does | Here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dune: Part One, the great ship over the fjord | a mass fills the top of the frame; light shows under it and land on both sides, so it hangs | the kingdom overhead, the band of day under its rim |
| Dune: Part One, the approach to the city by night | from the air, every line on the ground runs to one lit monument on the horizon | the mist lane, the stacks and the cutter's course all lead to the gates |
| Arrival, the shell over the water | the object small, the horizon in the upper third, one streak of light | horizon one third down; most of the frame is plain and haze |
| Blade Runner 2049, the city through the windscreen | a pattern from the air is legible near and gone in haze far off | the answer to the tabletop |
| Blade Runner 2049, the craft in fog | a craft from behind, nearly lost in one colour | the cutter the one clean thing in cold grey |
| Dune: Part One, the landing field | size told by small people at the foot of huge plain shapes | the crewman; the gates |

James Cameron's rule for the same frame: a small known thing (a man in a boat) beside a huge one.

## Why a plate first

So the cutter is put into the plate itself, from its sheet, and the take is asked only to move what is already there.
