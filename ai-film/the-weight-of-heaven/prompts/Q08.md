## The scene, as written

"An old fort on the lip of the plain, older than either flag. Serath steps from the cutter onto the ground. Her knees almost go. Behind her the aide falls outright. The gold collar drags at her neck like a hand. Surface soldiers watch the sky people learn to stand. Nobody laughs. The yard is full of refugees, grey with rock dust. A woman in armour beaten from imperial hull-plate is handing out spears: MARL... She puts one into the farmer's hands. He is alone. His free hand goes to the child's wooden bowl at his belt, and stays there. They both watch the admiral pass... KARST waits at the door. Fifties. Ink on his fingers. An imperial engineer's coat with the insignia cut away." His line in the cut: "We did not attack your city, Admiral. We stopped carrying it." (His two earlier lines and hers were cut from the film's script.)

Why two generations: the price is by the second, so two cost what one would. Each has fewer shots, so less can overrun; the line stands in a generation of two shots, where speech holds the timing (lesson 113: the last shot pays for the others' overruns).

Why this order in A: the landing has the most action and goes first, with the most time (lesson 44); Sarn Vale goes last, where a short return costs least, because the film already has a valley to fall back on.

**The collar shot does two jobs.** Its first second and a half is the collar. Its last two seconds, her head level and her eyes steady on someone to the left of the camera, can follow Karst's line in the cut as her answer: the screenplay gives her no reply, and the film's rule is that a hard thing lands on the face of the one it is said to (lesson 71).

Every shot is locked off: the film's rule is that the camera floats above and is planted below.

## Why plates

Lessons 118, 119, 124 and 125: a plate made for the shot comes back as the shot, and with the subject already in the plate the take only has to move what is there. The crossing of the plain was made that way and held first time. So each of the four new framings is made as a still first, with its people in it from their sheets, and is judged before any take is paid for. Sarn Vale needs none: its first frame is the film's own. Each plate is given only the piece of the yard its shot needs (the outer wall; the fort's wall; the door), cropped, so that the whole courtyard cannot come back behind it (lessons 111, 115).

## Known risks

- **A fall is the hardest thing asked of the model so far**: a knee that gives and is caught, and a man going down onto his hands and knees. It may come back as a stumble, a bow, or a slow kneel. It is timed to the half second and is the second priority of its take.
- **Which of Karst's hands is inked.** His approved sheet inks the left; the shot list says the right. The plate asks for "ink on his fingers, as in the sheet"; the author can settle it either way.
- **The plates put named faces into wide and medium frames from their sheets for the first time.** A face may drift from its sheet; each plate is judged against the sheet before its take, and the sheets go to the takes as well.
- **Counts and costume are what an image model gets wrong** (lesson 124): a second spear, armour too clean, a coat with its insignia still on. Mended by hand on a copy where it can be.
- **The line.** Eleven syllables and six, with a breath before and between, in five seconds, and a second and a half held after; if he hurries or adds a word the shot is lost. Nothing else in take B speaks, and no word of the line stands anywhere else in the prompt.
- **Sarn Vale may come back re-framed** against the frame in the film; it replaces that shot whole, so only the cut from the bird shot has to hold.
- **The model's sound** is kept for Karst's voice and the yard; the montage's song is still to be made and will sit over every shot but Karst's.
