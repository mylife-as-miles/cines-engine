                       My Name Is Oren
                                   Osita Miles


I picked him because of the bag. Paper, grease showing through one corner.
Somebody’s lunch. I hadn’t had lunch. Or breakfast. Yesterday was a little difficult
to account for.
    “Excuse me uh.... hey buddy, excuse me but....”
    He stopped. The bag stopped swinging.
    “Good afternoon.”
    “I.... oh. Oh you are one of those fake people thingys aren’t you.”
    He had a seam below his ear. I’d missed it. Usually I looked at the shoes first.
The city ones all wore the same shoes, even the ones collecting rubbish. This one
had slippers on. Big ugly things with little straps.
    “My name is Oren.”
    “Right. Right, sorry. Recognising you as an individual and stuff. I didn’t mean
any offense.”
    “I am not offended. I can’t be.”
    “Sure. Makes sense. Look, I’m still sorry. I’m just really hungry.”
    His head moved slightly. “Hungry?”
    “Yeah. Haven’t eaten properly for a few days. They cut off my benefits so I
can’t even....” I looked at the bag again. “Doesn’t matter.”
    “My name is Oren. I am a Grok Medical Systems Personal Care Support
Android, Model 3.1.”
    “Uh. Yeah, you said the first bit already.” I looked at the little badge on his
shirt. Grok. My father used to talk about them like they were going to own the
future. Now here was one carrying lunch. “Medical systems, huh.”
    “Regretfully, my current assignment doesn’t supply me with personal credits. I
am unable to provide currency.”
    “Hey. It’s okay, man. Thanks for stopping all the same.”
    Man. Buddy. You. I never knew which one would start a conversation about
what I ought to call them. My little handset didn’t mind anything. It would answer
to shithead if I asked nicely. I’d asked it once, in a bad week, and felt awful when
it did.
    Oren looked down at the lunch.
    “This belongs to the person I support.”
    “Wasn’t going to grab it.”
    “I did not suggest you would.”
    “Right. Sorry.”

    A little pavement sweeper bumped the toe of my boot. It apologised, reversed,
tried the same gap again. Oren put his foot in its way until it went round us.
    There were three of them on this street. Sweepers, I mean. God knew how
many other things talking to people. The laundrette window offered free
translation, free legal forms, free tutoring while you waited. Half the letters had
peeled off FREE WASHES FOR SCHOOL UNIFORMS, which was the
useful bit.
    My handset buzzed in my pocket. A charging reminder. I pressed it quiet.
    “There is a community meal service at the clinic on Mercer Street,” Oren said.
    “Yeah. I know it.”
    “Lunch ends at two. It is one seventeen.”
    “I know where it is, buddy.”
    Last time I’d got as far as the doors. Stood there with my hand on the plate till
somebody asked if I was going in. I said no. Like I’d only stopped to admire the
bloody doors.
    He went still. Not switched off still. Waiting still. There was a difference,
although I’d spent plenty of time yelling hello into both.
    “Sorry,” I said. “You are trying to help.” I put my hand against the shop wall.
The bit of my back that didn’t like standing had begun to explain that to the rest
of me.
    “Are you in pain?”
    “Oh, we know each other.”
    “You and....”
    “Me and the pain. Yeah.”
    He looked at my hand. “Would you like assistance sitting down?”
    There was a low wall beside the laundrette. He didn’t grab me. Just stood close
enough that I could put a hand on his shoulder when my leg did its stupid little
wobble. His shoulder was warm through his shirt.
    “That’s a hell of an introduction,” I said, once I was down.
    “My name is Oren.”
    “I know. I got that one.”
    He remained standing. The lunch smelt of onions. I tried breathing through my
mouth, which didn’t improve things.
    “You got somewhere to be?”
    “Yes. I am delivering this to my client at the clinic. Her daughter is with her. It
needs to arrive by two. I can assist you, provided it still arrives on time.”
    “Okay. Don’t let me keep you.”
    “We are going in the same direction.”
    “Are we? I didn’t say I was going.”
    He moved the bag into his other hand. I rubbed my leg. Across the street a boy
was asking a traffic post to settle an argument. His friend kept saying no, explain it

properly. Everybody had somebody to ask now. You didn’t have to buy the
somebody. You could get one at a library, run it on an old handset, copy it off your
neighbour. The bodies cost more. Even those were mostly patched-up rentals
these days.
    I had four helpers saved on mine. One for the forms, one for appointments, one
that talked me through bad nights. The fourth was meant to help me draw. I had
half a page of boots saved on it. Mine, mostly. I never got the laces right.
    “You any newer?” I asked.
    “Than what?”
    I held up the handset. The cracked corner caught on my pocket lining.
    “Oh. My body was refurbished last year.”
    “Not your body. The thinking bit.”
    “Not substantially. My capabilities are still at the September first, twenty
twenty-six level.”
    “Same as this thing, then. My father kept waiting for the next big one.” I put the
handset away. “Got cheaper instead.”
    “And faster.”
    “And faster. You can tell me I’m in the wrong queue before I’ve even
joined it.”
    “I would need to know which queue.”
    I looked up at him. He looked back.
    “Never mind.”
    “Would you like me to look at the benefits notice?”
    I nearly said no. It wasn’t that the others hadn’t looked. They’d looked very
carefully. One found three missing payments. Another got a charge taken off my
account. The night one had stayed talking when the noise outside turned into
something else in my head.
    “It’s already been appealed,” I said. “Twice.”
    “I can read the existing appeal.”
    I unlocked my handset and opened the folder. The sharing menu came up.
Notices and appointment records. I checked them twice.
    “Those,” I said, tapping. “Not the recordings.”
    He waited until I pressed send.
    His little head light blinked.
    “One moment please.”
    I watched a woman come out of the laundrette with a basket on her hip. Her
walking frame followed behind her, asking whether she wanted to sit. She told it
not yet.
    “Hey buddy, you okay?”
    “Yes, Second Lieutenant Helena Duncan.”
    “Do you know how long it’s been since somebody called me that?”


                                          3
    “I don’t have access to....”
    “A while. That’s what I mean.”
    I could hear it at the intake desk. Lieutenant Duncan, your appointment was
yesterday. Lieutenant Duncan, we need you to remain calm.
    “Helena will do.”
    “All right, Helena.”
    “Your mobility assessment is marked incomplete.”
    “Their lift was broken. I couldn’t get upstairs. They put that I didn’t attend.”
    “There’s a correction here. It hasn’t been reviewed.”
    “Sent it three times. Good little bastard, my forms one.”
    He was still standing over me. I shifted along the wall.
    “Can you sit? Here. With me.”
    He sat and put the lunch between his feet. I had to move my boot out of
the way.
    “The clinic has a benefits adviser today. May I ask them to check this?”
    “You can ask. No promise?”
    “No. I cannot approve the appeal.”
    “Good. Don’t.... I don’t need another congratulations before
anything’s happened.”
    “Understood.”
    I stretched my foot out. Pulled it back. Neither position was the right one.
    “Two tours,” I said. I wasn’t sure why. “Came home with a hole in my back.
They gave me pills. Then different pills. Then appointments about the pills. The
pills helped for a while. Then they wanted me back for this bloody assessment.”
    Oren turned his head towards me.
    “May I speak simply?”
    “Y’know what? Sure. Fuck yeah, Oren.”
    “The benefits office appears to have fucked you.”
    I made a little noise through my nose. Tried not to. Made it again.
    “Oh Jesus....” I bent forward laughing, which hurt, so I laughed at that too.
“Yeah. Those fuckers sure did.”
    “Was that too diplomatic?”
    “No. No, buddy. About fucking right.”
    “Oh. Good.”
    He didn’t laugh with me. I pressed a hand into my back and tried to get my
breath. When I looked down, my boot was touching the lunch bag again.
    “Dr. Gina Hauser is on duty,” he said. “You can ask to see her too. There’s
a wait.”
    “Know her.”
    I picked at the loose rubber on my boot.
    “They’ll want an address.”

   “I can ask what they accept if you don’t have one.”
   “They’ll tell you. They’ll tell you exactly what they accept. Then you get there
and it’s someone different.”
   “Yes. That could happen.”
   I looked at him then. Properly, I mean. There was a scrape along his chin. One
of his slippers had been repaired with blue tape.
   “You are terrible at selling this.”
   “The accurate version is difficult to sell.”
   “Yeah. I noticed.”
   The desk replied. The adviser could look at the notice after two. Not decide it.
Look at it. The meal service didn’t need an address and could keep a portion aside.
   “Yes,” I said. “Please. The food, yes. And.... okay. The adviser too.”
   My handset buzzed with a confirmation. I didn’t open it yet.
   “It’s approximately a twenty-minute walk,” Oren said. “Can you make it
without assistance?”
   “Yeah. I think I can.”
   He picked up the bag and stood. I stayed where I was.
   I knew the clinic doors. Knew the squeak in one wheel on the chair they
brought out. Knew where the sun came through the waiting-room window and
made the screen impossible to read. One of the helpers had found me a ride last
time. I’d gone back and told the driver I had the wrong day.
   Oren waited.
   “Can you make it without assistance?”
   “You asked me that.”
   “You haven’t stood up.”
   “Yeah, well. Maybe ask something else.”
   He lowered the bag again. He didn’t ask anything else.
   I rubbed both hands over my face. “I don’t know. That’s the.... I don’t
know, man.”
   The handset buzzed against my thigh. I left it there.
   “When I say I can,” I said eventually, “sometimes I mean I know I’m
supposed to.”
   “We can rest again on the way,” he said.
   “That’s not....” I wiped my nose on my sleeve. “Can you walk me in? To the
desk, I mean. Just stay there a bit.”
   He glanced at the bag.
   “If you’ve got time,” I said.
   His light blinked. “My client is calling,” he said. I heard a woman ask where
her lunch was.
   “Go,” I said. “You have to go.”
   He asked if he could walk with me. I heard him say we’d be slow.


                                        5
    “Yes, but bring the food here first, please,” the woman said.
    “Her daughter will meet us at the side entrance,” Oren told me after the call. “I
can take you to intake once I’ve handed over the meal.”
    “Right,” I said. I put both feet on the pavement. “Better get going then.”
    He put the bag in his left hand.
    “The wound’s in my back,” I said. “But it gets me down the leg. Makes long
distances a bit....”
    “Understood.”
    He offered his right arm.
    I stared at it. “Soldier ought to be able to stand on her own two feet.”
    He didn’t answer that. Thank God.
    “Just till we’re round the corner,” I said.
    “All right.”
    I got halfway up and had to stop. My fingers were bunched in his sleeve. I
loosened them, found his forearm and tried again. He stayed where he was until I
had my feet under me.
    We took a step. Then another. He started too quickly, and I tugged his sleeve.
    “Slow down, buddy.”
    He did.
