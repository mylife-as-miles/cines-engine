## The world

The near future, on an ordinary English side street. Machines are everywhere and always polite. Kerb sweepers apologise. Traffic posts settle arguments in calm, measured tones. The laundrette window offers free translation, free legal forms and free tutoring while you wait. None of it feeds anyone. Beside the new decals, an older sign with half its letters peeled away reads FREE WASHES FOR SCHOOL UNIFORMS: the last kindness a person put up by hand.

## The people

- **Helena Duncan**, 30s. A former Second Lieutenant with two tours behind her, who came home with a hole in her back. Her benefits were cut after a mobility assessment she couldn't reach: the lift was broken and they marked her as not attending. She hasn't eaten properly for days. She is proud and funny, and she jokes before anyone can pity her. On a cracked handset she draws her own boots, which are falling apart.
- **Oren**, a care android ("the person I support"). At a glance he passes for a man. Up close there is a fine seam below his ear, and a small light wakes under the skin at his temple when he talks to the network. He is not regulation issue: he wears big ugly strapped slippers, one mended with blue tape, and has a scrape along his chin. He is literal and accurate, and he can't sell anything. His stillness is the performance: "Not switched-off still. Waiting still."
- **The client**, a voice through Oren's speaker. She is never seen. **The street**: three kerb sweepers, two city units (synthetic, in hi-vis and grey regulation shoes, never seen above the chest) and foot traffic.

## What it is about

Every system in this film is polite, and none of it helps. Oren is part of the system: he can't approve the appeal, add to it or promise anything. What he can do is the smallest human thing. He stands close enough to be leaned on, he sits when he's asked, and he slows down. The film's one real decision is his. The client tells him "Two o'clock"; Helena tells him "Slow down." The light blinks once, and he slows down. We never learn what it costs him.

## How it is shot: feet first

The script tells its story through feet: regulation shoes, her failing boots, his slippers, a slipper held in front of a sweeper, a boot touching the lunch bag. **The camera starts at pavement level and has to earn faces.** We meet Helena by her boots and Oren by his slippers, and we see his seam only when she looks up at him. When she "looks at him properly, this time" (p. 6), the camera is at eye level with both of them in one frame for the first time. The last shot goes back to their feet: two pairs walking at her pace, and the bag swinging in time with her.

- **Two camera grammars.** Helena is filmed handheld, so the frame breathes with her. Oren is filmed locked-off: when he is waiting, the camera is still too. On the walk at the end the camera moves at her pace, not his.
- **One axis.** The camera stays on the street side, with the laundrette and the low wall behind them. The clinic and the corner are always frame right, and the walk always runs left to right.

## Cinematography: naturalistic, with Villeneuve's eye

*Chosen by the author.* The film is everyday and close, shot with Denis Villeneuve's precision. There are two grammars, and the story brings them together.

- **Helena is filmed like a social-realist film.** The camera is handheld, close and at eye level, breathing with her in natural light. The story is told through body details: boots, hands, the cracked handset. This is Andrea Arnold's closeness (*Fish Tank*), Clio Barnard's tenderness in northern light (*The Selfish Giant*), and Lynne Ramsay's feet, hands and fabric (*Ratcatcher*).
- **The city and Oren are filmed like Villeneuve.** Frames are locked-off and symmetrical, with slow, deliberate push-ins. Small figures stand against big flat walls and streets with empty space around them. The light is soft and overcast, and one accent colour (hi-vis yellow) sits in a muted world. *Prisoners* shows an ordinary grey street made monumental; *Arrival*, quiet contact with a non-human in muted grey light; *Blade Runner 2049*, a synthetic person filmed with dignity and stillness.
- **The grammars meet.** As she trusts him, the handheld settles. When she "looks at him properly" (B21), the camera is still, at eye level, with both of them in one frame. The last shot is a Villeneuve wide, two small figures turning the corner of an indifferent street, and it ends at the corner, before the doors.

These films are borrowed for their grammar, never imitated. No shot, frame or character is copied, and no frame from them is ever used as a reference image. Each is named in a prompt only with its purpose.

## Motifs, played without comment

- **The bag.** It swings with his stride, then stops. It hangs level with her face and sits between his feet; her boot touches it twice. At the end it swings again, slower, in time with her.
- **The light at his temple.** It wakes whenever the network is involved. Its last single blink is the choice.
- **"My name is Oren."** It is said three times: as a correction, as reassurance, and at last as a joke between them ("I know. I got that one.").
- **The clock.** 1:31, 1:36, two o'clock, ten past two: spoken, never shown on screen.
- **Hands.** Her hand stays on his shoulder a beat too long. Then her fingers bunch in his sleeve, then they find his forearm, and at the corner she doesn't let go.

## What the film never shows

We never see the client or her daughter, the clinic or the benefits office. We never see inside Oren: no heads-up display, no machine-vision point of view, no glowing eyes, nothing mechanical under the skin beyond the seam. Oren never laughs. There is no music under the dialogue.

## Sound

The film opens over black on polite synthetic voices, chimes and a traffic post settling an argument. The street's sound bed is courteous machines. Oren never takes a breath between phrases. Silence is used on purpose for his "waiting still" holds. There is no music until the last blink; then one small cue carries them round the corner and into the title.

## Ending

They turn the corner together, cut to black, and the super "MY NAME IS OREN" fades out. We don't see whether he is late or what the client says.
