# AUTOPILOT: three looks, one brief

*Autopilot* is a short film by Osita Miles: `design/screenplay.txt` (about 11 pages, so roughly 11 minutes on screen). Read it all before drawing anything.

Daniel, 38, leaves his phone charging by the kettle, an ordinary slip. On the hottest day of the year he drives his daughter Emily, 4, toward nursery. She leans out of the sun into the shade behind his headrest, and in his rear-view mirror her face disappears. He drives to work on autopilot. The phone is on the counter, so nothing reminds him. At 5:37 P.M. he drives home. Sarah asks: "Where's Emily?" At the nursery, the front door has been boarded up since the night before, so he cannot have been there. He turns toward his car. *We do not see inside.* "My phone was on the counter. And my daughter was dead. Autopilot disengaged."

The film's engine is the audience making Daniel's mistake with him. We lose Emily from the frame when he loses her from the mirror, and, like him, we don't notice until "Where's Emily?". Every look has to support that: after the mirror moment, the film must let the audience forget her as easily as he does.

## Non-negotiable (every look)
- **Restraint.** Emily is never shown in the car after the mirror moment. The interior of the rear seat is never visible in the final scene. There are no heat injuries, no body, no distress, no rabbit lying in the seat. The screenplay says "We do not see inside", and we don't. Grief is carried by Daniel, the door, the light and the silence. Nothing in these frames may be gratuitous.
- **Verbatim.** Any dialogue or V.O. that appears is word for word from the screenplay.
- **Craft.** These are premium film stills: a strong focal point, deliberate negative space, foreground, midground and background depth, one motivated light per shot. No clip-art, no generic stock look.
- **Code only.** Canvas 2D in HTML, rendered by the kit's headless browser. No image generators and no external images. Deterministic: never use `Math.random` or `Date`; use `OTM.rng(seed)`, `OTM.vnoise` and `OTM.fbm`.

## The three moments (draw the same three in every look, so they compare)
1. **THE COUNTER** (INT. KITCHEN, morning). The phone plugged in beside the kettle, its battery icon glowing. Daniel is leaving the frame, heading upstairs, back turned. His work bag waits by the front door, its outside pocket empty. "The phone remains on the counter." This is the film's key object; it opens and closes the story.
2. **THE MIRROR** (INT. CAR, morning). The rear-view mirror in the foreground shows the back seat. Emily is in her child seat behind the driver, with her rabbit (one ear worn almost flat). A hard white strip of sun cuts across where her face was; she is leaning into the shade behind Daniel's headrest, and in the mirror she is disappearing, half gone. This image explains the whole film. She is alive and sleepy here; it is tender, not ominous.
3. **THE DOOR** (EXT. NURSERY CAR PARK, dusk). Daniel kneels beside the car's open rear door, one arm extended into the car. The door and his body hide the interior completely. The sun sinks out of the glass. The swings' long shadows reach across the tarmac from the silent playground. The world has fallen SILENT. This is the hardest frame: dignity and restraint, with all the grief in his posture and the light.

## Characters
- **Daniel**, 38: kind-faced, worn thin by long days, a parent's tiredness. Shirt and tie; the collar sits crooked in the morning (Emily straightens it).
- **Emily**, 4: sleepy eyes, uneven socks, a soft rabbit with one ear worn almost flat. Child proportions: a big head and a short body. She is the warmth of the film.
- **Sarah**, 36, and **Priya**, early 30s, appear later (not needed in these three frames).

## The three looks

**A: WHITE HEAT.** Cut paper in daylight: a sister to the Negative Space look of *One Too Many*, but the light is the antagonist. The sun is drawn as hard white planes that eat into the paper. Through the day the world bleaches toward white, shadows shrink at noon and stretch copper at dusk, and only the office stays refrigerated blue-green. Figures are flat paper cut-outs with rim light. Faces are reduced to a few precise cuts (eyes and mouth as slits of light or shadow), never cartoon. Emily owns one soft colour (rose: her socks, her rabbit) that appears nowhere else. After the mirror the film carries no rose at all, and its absence is the clue nobody notices. The only other accent is the phone's green charging pulse. Time stamps (6:14 A.M., 8:32 A.M., 5:37 P.M.) are set as large, cold type.

**B: BRIGHT DAY.** The idiom of the big story-animation channels on YouTube: simple round-headed characters in solid near-black with big, expressive white eyes. The worlds are bright, saturated and flat, with soft cel shading, chunky clean shapes and a cheerful, readable staging. Emily is a small round character with big sleepy eyes, pigtails, mismatched socks and a pink rabbit; Daniel is tall and slightly hunched, with a crooked collar and a loose tie. It is warm and inviting, which is what makes it devastating. The colour drains out of the world from "Where's Emily?" onward, and the final scene is a desaturated violet dusk where the only colour left is the phone's green pulse in the flash. This look is the one built for reach, but it must keep the ending dignified.

**C: THROUGH GLASS.** Every shot is framed through a pane: the phone's screen, the kettle's chrome, steam on the shower glass, the rear-view mirror, the windscreen, the office's sealed window, the laptop screen, and finally the dark rear window where Daniel sees only his own reflection. The grammar is frame within frame: the film is about what we look at and what we don't. Render it as graphic realism with flat shapes, glare streaks, reflections and a little refraction, in a muted naturalistic palette (desaturated teal and amber), letterboxed to 2.39:1. THE DOOR can be seen through the glass of the open door itself, with Daniel beyond it, kneeling, his arm reaching into the car past the edge of frame.
