You have just been born. Welcome.
Before you even take a proper breath, / I need to tell you something.
You have also just been hired.
No interview. No contract. No salary.
And no resignation letter.
Because this job does not have one.
You are the firstborn.
And in an African family, / that is not a position.
It is a career.
So let's walk through your entire life.
Stay with me, because by the end,
somebody in your family might be / about to call you for something.
Right now, your parents have no idea / what they are doing.
Zero. But they are very serious about it.
You sneeze once, and it is an emergency.
They carry you to the hospital / with a bag like they are relocating.
Three years later, your little brother / sneezes the same sneeze.
"Give him water."
You are version 1.0, full of bugs,
and your parents are fixing you, / live, in production.
And the rules. Oh, the rules.
No sweets, no biscuits before dinner, / bed at seven.
Your younger sibling will be eating / biscuits at midnight in front of the TV
and nobody will say a word.
You were the experiment. / They were the scientists.
Your siblings get the stable release.
Then a baby arrives.
And your mother looks at you. / You know the look.
You are six years old / and you have just been promoted.
"Carry your sister. Watch your brother. / Don't let him touch that."
You are six. / You can barely carry your own school bag.
Now, here is the rule of the house. / It is very simple.
If the small one cries, you did it.
You say, "I didn't even touch him."
They say, "Then why is he crying?"
That is it. That is the whole case.
No evidence. Judgment already passed.
And when you are really in trouble, / listen for it.
"First name. Middle name. Surname."
By the time they reach the surname, / you are already apologising
and you still do not know the crime.
"Go and buy salt." You go. You come back.
"Where is the change?"
You give it, all of it, to the last kobo, / because this family audits.
And when your younger one misbehaves,
"Look at your big brother. / Did he do this?"
You are standing right there. / You are now a poster.
A moving, breathing, / water-carrying poster.
Now you are a teenager, and your grades / do not belong to you anymore.
They belong to the family.
Your parents' friends know your results / before you do.
An auntie calls from another state.
"I heard you came first."
How? You have not even told anyone.
And the careers? / You have exactly three choices.
Doctor, lawyer, engineer.
Four, if the family / is feeling adventurous,
and the fourth one is also doctor.
You say, / "I would like to do something creative."
The room goes silent.
Somebody has to go and hold your mother.
And of course, / there is the neighbour's child.
You have never spoken to this child,
but this child is somehow / in your house every single day.
"Mrs. Okafor's son is already / reading in his sleep."
Good for him.
Here is the part nobody says out loud.
Your younger siblings are watching.
If you succeed, they have a path. / If you fail, they have a warning.
So you cannot fail.
You are not studying for yourself.
You are studying for / the whole queue behind you.
You graduate. You get a job.
First salary alert.
You feel like a champion, / you open your banking app,
and for exactly four minutes, / you are happy.
Then the calls start.
"My dear, I don't want to disturb you."
That is how it always starts.
They do not want to disturb you, / then the disturbance arrives.
School fees. Rent. A hospital bill. / "Just a small thing."
Some people call it black tax.
Whatever you call it, the invoice is real, / and nobody asked if you have a budget.
Congratulations, / you are now the family bank.
No interest, no collateral, / and no customer care.
You say, "But who is paying my own rent?"
And somebody says, "God will provide."
And he does. / But somehow, he keeps using your account.
And the funny thing is, you do it. / Because you love them.
When your younger sister walks / across that graduation stage,
the one you paid for, / you will cry harder than she does.
Even if you are pretending / it is dust in your eye.
By 30, your job title has changed again.
You are now management.
At every wedding, every funeral, / every naming ceremony,
you are somehow in charge of something.
Somebody turns to you and says, / "Who is handling the rice?"
"Why do I know about rice? / I came here to attend."
Uncles who have not spoken in five years / will only talk through you.
Two cousins are fighting, / and you are the United Nations.
And then there is the question / that has followed you since you were 25.
"So, when are we eating your rice?"
Not if. When.
Now, let's be honest for a minute.
All of this is funny, and it is,
but if you are a firstborn, / you are also tired.
And there is a reason this role exists.
It is not that you are the strongest. / It is that you were first.
That is all. Position, not talent.
Your parents did not test you and decide / you were the most capable.
They were scared. They were learning.
And you were the one standing closest.
Here is the part worth knowing.
A lot of that pressure is love,
said in a language that does not know / how to say it softly.
"Be responsible" often means / "we are afraid."
"Don't disappoint us" often means / "we are counting on you
because we do not know / who else to count on."
You can understand that and still say, / "This is too heavy."
So here is something / you can do this month.
Pick one thing you carry.
One bill, one phone call, / one decision that always lands on you.
Say it out loud to the family.
"This is now shared." / Not as a fight, as a plan.
Give it a name and give it a person.
You would be surprised how many people / were waiting to be asked.
So that is your whole life.
Born, hired, promoted, never paid.
If you are a firstborn, / tell me in the comments.
What is the most responsible thing / you have ever been asked to do?
I want to see who has been / carrying the whole house.
And if you are the baby of the family / laughing right now and thinking,
"I don't know what she is talking about,"
I know. That is the problem.
Call your elder sibling today.
Don't ask for anything.
Just say, "How are you?"
And watch what happens.
They might not even know what to say.
See you in the next one.
