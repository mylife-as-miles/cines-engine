# The Prize That Became a Prison: script, version 2 (cinematic)

Written 27 November 2026 at the author's request: "Don't stick exactly with the pdf script but use it as inspiration
and foundation... Even the direction... I want this to be high hook, engaging and intense", and more cinematic than
fern's films. The locked PDF of 5 November (`Dawn_Hunt_Script.pdf`) stays the factual foundation: every fact,
number, date and attribution below comes from it and its source ledger (S1–S9). Nothing is invented. Where version 2
says less than the PDF, it is on purpose; where it says something new, it is structure, not fact.

About 1,700 spoken words, about 12:55 with the holds marked: the cold open at about 160 words a minute, easing to about
135 by the end (the PDF: 2,015 words, 15:28 at 138). Shorter on purpose: every chapter now moves the story, and the
last four minutes have room to be silent. Ten chapters (the YouTube chapters).

Rules from the second study of the two reference films (27 November 2026, `design/REFERENCES.md`, "Hook craft"),
kept here because they set the edit:
- **A sound drop before every reveal**: 0.5–3 s at −30 LUFS or quieter before each turn (the fee, YES, the linings,
  the sentence, "Deceived", Gene's death, "It was hers"), at least once every 90 s.
- **Title on the peak of a 10 s flash-forward montage** at 0.5–0.8 s a shot, then the score decays into silence.
- **Chapters end on a flash-forward or a named threat** of 15 words or fewer, then 1.5–2 s of silence; the next
  chapter's title is a small super over its establishing shot, never a card.
- **Cut 8–15 times a minute after 3:00**; no hold over 12 s except the planned silences.
- **Plants paid on screen**: the fee and the scale, YES, the emails (sent in chapter 1, handed to investigators in
  chapter 5), the bags' position in frame (cold open, chapter 4, the end), Gene's chair (full in chapter 1, empty in
  chapter 8), the unanswered question (cold open, chapter 9).
- **Voice**: 150–165 words a minute in exposition, 120–135 in the last three chapters; about a fifth of the lines six
  words or fewer. **Mix**: −14 LUFS integrated with a loudness range of 7 LU or more (the references are flat at 4.8–6).
- **No invented thoughts, no speculation, no moral at the end** (the references' weakest moments).

Picture rules that make it more cinematic than the references:
- **Every scene change is a match cut** through something both sides share: the scale display, the bags, the chair,
  the beam of light, the word YES. Registered in the same place in frame on both sides (the author's rule).
- **One camera move per shot, always motivated**: a slow push on the evidence, a slow pull back on the prison years,
  locked frames for the room and the verdict. Nothing floats without reason.
- **Light tells the story**: warm Chicago and Hong Kong; cold fluorescent from the check-in desk on; the Void Stage's
  single beam for the sentence and the years. The red accent appears only on the seam: once for a second in the cold
  open, then traced in full in chapter 3, never again.
- **Type is part of the picture** and always says what the voice does not: dates, places, the scale's word, YES,
  WHEN?. Never a label, never a source credit.
- **Restraint at the worst moments**, as the references do and further: no reenacted assault, no invented
  photograph, no voice pretending to be Dawn, Tim, an officer or a judge. Her face is never shown suffering.

Stages and cameras named in the direction are the ones built or being built (`scenes/stages/`). Cameras not yet built
are marked *new*.

## 00 · Cold open: Overweight (0:00–1:30)

**VOICE (present tense, close, quick)**

The bags are overweight.

Only slightly. There's a small fee, and she pays it without a second thought.

It's 2014, at an airport in China. Dawn Michelle Hunt, an American from Chicago, is checking two bags for the next
flight of a trip she won.

*(beat)*

Not long after, security staff call her aside. She is led into a room, and her two bags are already there.

The officers want to know one thing. Are these bags hers?

According to her brother, she says yes.

They ask again.

Yes.

Why would she say anything else? She packed them herself. She just paid to carry them.

*(silence, 1 s)*

Inside the luggage are designer handbags. And sewn into the linings of those handbags, authorities say, is more than
two kilograms of methamphetamine.

Her family says she had no idea it was there. She believed the handbags were a prize, from the people who had paid
for the trip of her life.

The luggage was hers. The drugs, she said, were not.

*(score cut dead; silence, 1.5 s)*

Three years later, a court sentences her to death, with a two-year reprieve. Today, she is serving life.

And twelve years after that airport, her brother appears before American lawmakers with a question that no one in
the room can answer.

*(no voice: a 10 s montage of what is coming, the score building to its peak; the title lands on the peak and the
score decays into silence, 5 s)*

## 01 · The winner (1:30–3:05)

**VOICE**

Before China, there was Chicago.

Dawn was a daughter and a sister. A former flight attendant. An artist who loved fashion, and loved to travel.

Her brother Tim remembers her as intelligent, and trusting. She and their mother liked to enter sweepstakes, he told
Reuters. For Dawn, every entry was a small possibility that something wonderful might happen.

In 2014 she was working as a receptionist, in a family that had given decades to public service. Her father, Gene,
had served in the Army and then as a Chicago police sergeant. Tim had been a Chicago police officer himself.

A family that knew what danger looked like.

What arrived in Dawn's inbox didn't look like danger at all.

It looked like she had won.

*(beat)*

The message said she'd won a contest. Accounts differ on exactly what the prize was: cash, travel, or both. But
every version has the same shape. An unexpected win, and people ready to fly her across the world.

Dawn didn't believe it. Not at first. Tim says she looked the organizers up online. What she found looked real
enough that she sent her passport details.

Then the thing that ended her doubts arrived.

The tickets.

Real tickets to Hong Kong, paid for by someone else.

That is what makes this story so hard to shake. The first part of the promise was true.

Her family believes the deception was hidden in everything that came after.

*(silence, 1.5 s)*

## 03 · The gift (4:00–5:05)

Overweight.

Tim put that detail in his testimony for a reason. To him, it shows how ordinary this journey felt to his sister.

The people behind the trip had given her the handbags. They had solved her luggage problem. And whatever was hidden

## 05 · Three years (6:20–7:25)

**VOICE**

For Dawn, the airport wasn't the end of a bad trip. It was the first day of a detention that would last about three
years before any verdict.

Her family says she tried to cooperate. Tim testified that she answered investigators' questions, and gave them the
emails she had received from the organizers. The same emails that had started it all.

It wasn't enough.

Her family also alleges something worse. In testimony to Congress in 2024, Tim said Dawn had told their father she
was sexually assaulted in the detention facility. That allegation has not been independently established in the
sources available to us.

We are not going to recreate it. It is serious enough to state plainly, and Dawn has already lost control of so much
of her own story.

For three years, her family waited for a trial to correct what they believed was a terrible mistake.

The verdict was worse than anything they had feared.

## 06 · The sentence (7:25–8:45)

**VOICE**

2017.

A death sentence, with a two-year reprieve.

The words matter. It was not an order for her immediate execution. It was a suspended death sentence, and it was
later reduced to life in prison.

China sees the case as closed. Asked about it in 2026, China's foreign ministry told Reuters that Dawn and another
imprisoned American were serving sentences for serious drug-related crimes, and that their legal rights and health
were being protected.

Her family sees a scam victim. They insist she was what investigators call a blind mule: someone who carries drugs
for traffickers without knowing it.

And this is the part they find hardest to accept. According to her family, and to reporting on the court's reasoning,
the judge recognised that Dawn had been deceived, and concluded that she should have known anyway.

Deceived.

And guilty anyway.

## 07 · The wait (8:45–10:10)

**VOICE**

Dawn is held at Guangdong Women's Prison. It's where her brother has gone to visit her.

In 2024, Tim told Congress that his sister had lost weight, and become pale. Prison doctors had found uterine
fibroids, and raised the possibility of ovarian cancer. A possibility, not a diagnosis. She'd had heavy bleeding, and
blood transfusions. Doctors recommended a hysterectomy. She refused, Tim said, in part because she did not trust the
people responsible for her care.

At home in Chicago, the family turned the case into a campaign. Tim wrote to officials and to lawyers. In September
2024, he testified before a congressional commission, and his sister's case became part of the public record.

In May 2025, according to Tim, the United States formally requested her release on humanitarian grounds. Members of
Congress introduced a bipartisan bill carrying her name, and the name of another American imprisoned in China,
Nelson Wells Junior.

Every letter, every hearing, every request was a door that might open.

None of them brought her home.

And the man who had waited longest was running out of time.

## 09 · When? (11:10–12:55)

**VOICE**

Six months later, in July 2026, Tim went back to the prison.

He told lawmakers he barely recognised his sister. She seemed frail. Stooped. Her teeth had changed. Her hair was
much greyer than two years before. She struggled to focus on their conversation.

But there was one thing she kept coming back to.

One question.

When would she be released?

Tim could not give her a date.

*(beat)*

In September 2026, he appeared before Congress again. There was no reunion to report. Only an empty place at the
family table, and a brother still fighting.

Remember the question at the beginning of this story? The one no one in that room could answer.

It was never really his question.

It was hers.

*(silence, 2 s)*

As of November 2026, Dawn Michelle Hunt is still in prison in China. Her family is still asking for her release on
humanitarian grounds.

The State Department's advice is simple: never carry gifts or luggage across a border for people you do not truly
know. Dawn thought she knew them. She had spent weeks in their company.

Twelve years ago, in a room at an airport, officers asked her a question. She answered it. Yes.

Now she is the one asking.

And no one has an answer.

*(the end card; voice, quiet)* The testimony, the reporting and her family's campaign are linked below.
