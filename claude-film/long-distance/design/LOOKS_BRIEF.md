# LONG DISTANCE: three looks, one brief

*Long Distance* is a short film by Osita Miles: `design/screenplay.txt` (7 pages, roughly 7 to 9 minutes on screen). Read it all before drawing anything. `design/DECISIONS.md` holds what the author has settled.

A man in his thirties lives alone in the flat he once shared with a woman. Every night he falls asleep talking to her on the phone, which lies on the smooth, unslept pillow where her head would be. They text good morning and I love you; on his lunch break she sends him pictures. One evening she sends a photo of the two of them in front of a waterfall, her long hair, her head tipped against his, exactly as on the photo-booth strip on his fridge. He makes it his wallpaper. Then there is a knock at the door. It is her: real, older, her hair cut short, coat on. She has come for an old makeup bag. While he fetches it, his phone buzzes on the couch, and she sees the lock screen: a message from a heart, "Call me tonight?", over a photo of them that was never taken. "Have you been using AI to make pictures of me?" "I just missed you, is all." She leaves. He touches the screen awake, opens the app with her old face on it, and asks, "Are you there?" At once, warm and close: "Yes."

The film's engine is the audience believing in the long-distance relationship with him. Nothing tells us she is not real until she is standing in the doorway. The clues are all in plain sight — the voice with no room behind it, the answers that arrive instantly, the dust on her toothbrush and her mug, the bare hangers, the pale rectangle on the wall, the waterfall photo lit exactly like the fridge strip — and every look must let them sit there unnoticed on a first watch and unmistakable on a second.

## Non-negotiable (every look)
- **Restraint.** The pictures she sends are never shown. In the stairwell we see only what they do to him (the breath going out, the colour in his neck); over his shoulder, the thread is an out-of-focus grid, "a blur of skin tones", abstract and unreadable. No nudity, no body, nothing sexual, ever. The woman is never drawn as an object to look at. Grief and shame are carried by his face, his hands, the rooms and the light.
- **Verbatim.** Any dialogue that appears is word for word from the screenplay. Subtitles name their speaker, as film subtitles do ("Him: …", "Her: …"). The phone's voice is labelled "Her:" too: the label must not give the twist away.
- **The two kinds of image.** The photo-booth strip on the fridge is a real photograph: cheap flash, grain, a crooked crop, four frames, the two of them squeezed in, laughing. The waterfall image and the app's portrait are generated: the same faces and the same pose, but subtly too even — the light falls on both faces the same way, the skin a little too smooth, the water too white. A first-time viewer should not notice; a second-time viewer should not be able to stop noticing. Never caricature the AI images.
- **Craft.** These are premium film stills: a strong focal point, deliberate negative space, foreground, midground and background depth, one motivated light per shot. No clip-art, no generic stock look.
- **Code only.** Canvas 2D in HTML, rendered by the kit's headless browser. No image generators and no external images (the "AI images" inside the story are drawn in code too). Deterministic: never use `Math.random` or `Date`; use `OTM.rng(seed)`, `OTM.vnoise` and `OTM.fbm`.

## The three moments (draw the same three in every look, so they compare)
1. **THE PILLOW** (INT. BEDROOM, night). A double bed: one side slept in, the other still made, the pillow smooth. He lies on his side facing the smooth pillow. The phone rests on it, where a head would be, screen turned away from us; its glow is on his face. His hand lies over the phone "the way you would touch a sleeping shoulder"; his eyes are closing. This image opens the film and returns, transformed, at its end (the couch). It must be tender, not pathetic.
2. **THE WATERFALL** (INT. APARTMENT, living room, night). Small, tidy for one, furnished for two: a pale rectangle on the wall where a frame used to hang, two mugs on the shelf (one with a ring of dust around its base). He stands eating from the pan, the phone propped against the salt. On the phone, the image we are allowed to see: the two of them in front of a waterfall, white water behind, her long hair, her head tipped against his. On the fridge behind him, under a magnet, the photo-booth strip: the same two faces, the same tilt of her head, in the last frame. Both images must read in the frame; the rhyme between them is the film's hidden clue. He has stopped chewing.
3. **THE SCREEN BETWEEN THEM** (INT. APARTMENT, living room, day, grey daylight). She holds his phone up between them, screen turned to face him: the waterfall, the long hair. She has her coat on, bag on her shoulder, her hair cut short above the jaw; her free hand has risen to the back of her neck, where her hair used to be. He stands with the old floral makeup bag in his hand, its zip broken, his thumb on the zip, not looking at the screen or at her. The room is the same room as moment 2, now in daylight. Subtitle in the picture: "Her: We’ve never taken this photo. We’ve never been to a waterfall."

## Characters
- **Him**, 30s: no name. Gentle, ordinary, kind-faced; a man who has kept everything exactly as it was. Small signs of neglect he does not see (a shirt worn one day too long, unopened lunch). Not a creep and not a monster: the film is about loneliness and refusal, and the audience must feel for him even when it understands.
- **Her**, the woman: in the old photographs (the photo-booth strip, the generated images) she has long hair past her shoulders and is laughing. In person she is older "by exactly the time that has passed": hair cut short above the jaw, coat kept on like a guest, composed, tired, not cruel. Her face in the AI images is her younger face. The rhyme and the difference between the two must be clear when we finally see her.
- Choose their features and keep them consistent across your three frames; the author will settle the cast with the look.
- Others (a coworker, bus passengers) are background only.

## The three looks

**A: THE GLOW.** A night film lit by screens. His world is deep, cool blue-black; the only warm light in his life is the phone, and it is the AI's light: amber-white, soft, close. Every shot is built around where the screen's glow falls — on his face on the pillow, on the salt cellar, on his neck in the stairwell, in the bus window. Graphic realism with flat shapes and soft light shafts, a night-time Hopper loneliness. Daylight is rare and grey; the visit is the first time the flat is lit by something that is not a screen, and in it the real woman looks almost colourless beside the phone's warm image. At the end, the phone's glow returns and he lies down inside it.

**B: GRAIN AND GLOSS.** Two textures for two kinds of truth. The real world is drawn with texture and imperfection: paper grain, hand-cut edges (a sister to *One Too Many*'s cut paper), lived-in clutter, uneven light, the photo-booth strip's harsh flash and noise. Everything the phone produces — the waterfall, the app's portrait, even the colour of her messages — is rendered a shade too smooth: clean gradients, perfect symmetry, no grain, no dust. On first watch it reads as a nice phone screen; on second, the difference is the whole film. When the real woman walks in, she has texture, and that is what makes her real.

**C: HALF EMPTY.** A strict compositional grammar: almost every frame is divided into two halves, one occupied by him, one empty — the made half of the bed, the second toothbrush, the bare hangers, the pale rectangle, the second mug — and the phone always sits in the empty half, where a person would be. Symmetrical, centred, still frames with flat colour fields and wide negative space; figures often small in the frame. When she arrives she fills the empty half for the first time — and what she holds up in it is the phone. In the last shot he lies down with the phone in the empty half again. Restrained, graphic, almost architectural; muted warm-grey palette with one cold accent for the screen.
