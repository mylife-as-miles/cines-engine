# Look C: HALF EMPTY

**Pitch.** Every frame is two squares, one for him and one left empty, and the phone always sits in the empty one, where she would be. When she finally walks into it, what she holds up there is the phone.

Style frames: `frames/C1_pillow.jpg`, `frames/C2_waterfall.jpg`, `frames/C3_between.jpg` (sources in `src/C*.html`, helpers in `kit/look_c.js`).

## The grammar

- **2.00:1, two squares side by side.** The frame is split down the middle, and the set supplies the seam: the gap between two pillows, the stitched seam of the headboard, the place where the kitchen tiles stop, a cupboard joint, a door frame. The line has to come from the architecture or it reads as a diagram.
- **His half and the empty half.** He is almost always in the left square. The right square is empty, or holds only the evidence: the made side of the bed, the second toothbrush, the bare hangers, the pale rectangle, the second mug. **The phone always sits in the empty square, where a person would stand or lie.**
- **Still, centred cameras.** Plans (looking straight down) and elevations (looking straight at a wall), long lenses, verticals kept vertical. There are almost no camera moves: the stillness is the loneliness.
- **Flat colour fields** in muted warm greys, shaded in two or three posterised steps (soft bedding is modelled as a height field and posterised, so folds come out as clean flat bands).
- **One cold colour.** Nothing in the film is cold except the screen and the light it throws. A cold patch on his face always means the phone.
- **The grammar breaks only twice.** On the packed bus no half is empty: strangers fill both squares. And when she walks in, the empty half is filled for the first time.

## Colour script

1. **Night bedroom (opening, C1).** A long lens high over the foot of the bed, dead centre. The room is warm dark grey; the bed is a pale island between two unlit lamps. His half of the quilt is rucked, its outer corner thrown back over a triangle of creased sheet. Her half is tight and square, the sheet turned down in a straight line. The only light is the phone, standing on her pillow with its back to us and its screen turned to his face; its cold light lands on his profile.
2. **Bathroom mornings.** An elevation of the basin and mirror in flat pale warm grey (`#C9C1B5`). The glass of two toothbrushes sits on the centre line; his blue one is lifted out of his half, hers stays grey with dust in the empty half. The phone is propped against the mirror on her side; the message is the only cold in the room. "I love you." arrives before his thumb leaves the glass.
3. **Grey office.** A wide, symmetrical elevation of open-plan dividers (`#8E877C`), with him small at his desk. When he turns the phone face down, the cold disappears from the film for the length of the shot.
4. **Concrete stairwell.** A plan straight down the stairwell's square spiral (`#7A746C`), with him small on one step and the unopened lunch on the next. The empty half is the empty flight below. The screen's cold falls only on his chin and neck; see Restraint.
5. **Evening bus.** The one crowded frame. Bodies fill both squares, coats in browns and greys, and the grammar is broken because he is not alone, only lonely. The cold is the crawling waveform. His ears take the film's one red (`#B5574A`), one flat step on the ear shape.
6. **Living room at night (C2).** An elevation of the kitchen wall under one warm pendant. His half is lit and tiled: he stands in profile at the counter, the pan held against his chest, the fork still, looking down at the phone with his mouth stopped on a mouthful. The photo-booth strip is on the fridge behind his back. Her half is dark plaster with the pale rectangle, two mugs, and the phone propped against the salt, turned to him and to us; it is the brightest and smoothest thing in the frame.
7. **The visit, grey daylight (C3).** The same wall on the same axis, the camera pulled back. The light now comes from a window on her side, and for the first time the empty half is the bright one. She stands in it, in front of the pale rectangle, in a rust coat, the only warm colour that has entered the flat from outside, and holds the phone out to him at the centre line with its screen turned to his face. He stands nearer us in his own half, turned away, head down over the bag. The phone's cold is weak in daylight, but it still lands on his face.
8. **The last image, the couch.** An elevation of the couch against the same wall at dusk. He lies along his half; the phone rests on the cushion in the empty half beside his face, where a head would be: C1 turned through ninety degrees. The daylight drains out until the screen is again the only light, and he lies down inside it. The strip on the fridge behind him stays in the room's last warm grey.

## Characters

- **Him.** Thirties, gentle, ordinary. Dark brown hair a few weeks past a haircut, a day's stubble, a soft jaw. An oat cotton shirt worn a day too long: open collar, sleeves rolled, creased across the back. He is drawn with the same profile in all three frames. He has deep brown skin and a heavy build in all three frames. In C1 he is a profile on the pillow seen from above the foot of the bed. In C2 he is in profile, his mouth stopped mid-chew. In C3 he is three-quarters, eyes down, brows lifted at the inner ends: shame, not menace. His hands carry it: the hand laid over the phone like a hand on a sleeping shoulder; the pan lowered and forgotten; the thumb on the broken zip.
- **Her, two ages, one face.** In the pictures (the strip, the waterfall, the app portrait) she is younger: long dark hair, centre-parted, past her shoulders, laughing. In person she is the same face, older by exactly the time that has passed: a blunt bob cut above the jaw and parted on the side, a little tiredness under the eyes, the mouth level and held. She keeps on a rust wool coat with a dark turtleneck, the bag strap across her. When she lifts her hand to her nape, only her fingers show, under the blunt ends of hair that used to fall there.
- **The two kinds of image.**
  - *The photo-booth strip is a print.* It takes the light of the room it is in: it is multiplied by the room's colour, warm under the pendant and grey by day, and it never glows. It has grain, cheap-flash falloff (hot foreheads, dark corners), a hard flash shadow on the curtain, a crooked crop in every frame, a little motion blur in frame 3, a curl at the bottom and a magnet.
  - *The waterfall and the app's portrait are generated, and they make their own light.* They are drawn into the emissive layer, so they halate. There is no grain anywhere in them, the composition is mirror-symmetrical, one soft key lights both faces identically with no shadow, the skin is a pure gradient and the water is pure white. It is also rendered more smoothly than anything else in the film: softened, with an even halo on both heads. The faces and the pose are those of the strip's last frame, her head tipped against his.
  - On a first watch it is a nice photo on a bright phone. On a second, the strip is the only picture in the film that is lit by the room, and you cannot stop seeing it.

## Screens, messages, subtitles

- **Screens are objects in the set, never overlays.** The phone's content is always mapped onto the glass in true perspective, inside the shot. When a message must be read, the shot is an insert that keeps the grammar: the phone fills one square, and the other square is empty table, sink or cushion.
- **Messages** are plain grey and white bubbles with no app branding. The contact is a single heart, ♥, as its name. The lock screen has a thin clock, the date and one banner, and the waterfall wallpaper never changes after he sets it.
- **Subtitles** are set in Libre Franklin at 40 px (1080p), white at full strength on a soft dark backing with a shadow, so they read on any background. There are at most two lines, centred at the bottom inside the 2.00:1 picture. Each subtitle opens with its speaker's name in bold, as film subtitles do: "Him: …", "Her: …". The phone's voice is labelled "Her:" exactly like the woman, so the label never gives the twist away.

## How the twist stays hidden, then lands

The grammar is the disguise. For the first two-thirds of the film, the phone placed in the empty half reads as the oldest film convention for separation: she is far away, so her half is empty and only her voice is there. The audience accepts it as a long-distance relationship because the frames are built like one.

Every clue sits inside that same grammar, in the empty half, in plain sight. The second toothbrush is grey with dust and the second mug has a ring of dust around it. The hangers on her side are bare, a rectangle of wall is pale where a frame used to hang, and the generated waterfall faces the real strip across the centre line. Answers arrive before the cut. Nothing points at any of it; it is simply in the half the audience has learned to read as hers.

The landing is a change of light, not a reveal. Her visit is shot on the same wall, on the same axis, as the waterfall night, but the light now comes from her side. Then the one rule of the film breaks: a person stands in the empty half. And what she holds up in it is the phone, and on it is her own younger face. The substitution is shown literally, with no explanation. After she leaves, the last shot puts the phone back in the empty half beside his head, and the audience reads the convention they trusted for seven minutes in reverse.

## The stairwell's restraint

The pictures she sends are never in frame. The stairwell is a plan, straight down the concrete well: he is small on one step, the lunch unopened beside him, the empty flights below.

When the chime comes, he tilts the phone to his chest and the shot stays wide and high. All we get is what it does to him, drawn in two flat steps of colour. The screen's cold lights his chin from below. One warm step (`#B5574A` at low strength) rises on the shape of his neck. His breath shows as a lift and drop of the shoulders.

The over-the-shoulder shot of the thread is a grid of out-of-focus rectangles in four muted warm greys, blurred past any reading. It contains no figure, no shape and no pink, and the blur is applied before the colour, so nothing can be recovered by sharpening. The thread scrolls and keeps coming, a rhythm and nothing more.

## What it would cost to build all 7 to 9 minutes

- **Why it is cheap to animate.** It is the most economical of the three looks. The cameras are locked off and the sets are elevations or plans, each drawn once per room and reused for every shot in it. The living room and kitchen wall alone carries the waterfall night, the visit, the argument and the last image. Most of the motion is small and exact: breathing, blinks, the screen's light switching on and dimming, a thumb on a zip, a hand rising to the nape, the phone turned to face him.
- **What exists.** `kit/look_c.js` already has the shared pieces: a pinhole camera with plan and elevation set-ups, posterised height-field bedding, flat light bands, the screen as a light, the phone in 3D with perspective-correct screen mapping, both kinds of image, the lock screen and the photo view, and subtitles.
- **Sets: about 9 days.** Eight sets at roughly one day each (bedroom, bathroom, office, stairwell, bus, the living-room wall, the doorway, the wardrobe), plus the insert screens.
- **Character rigs: about 6 days.** Him in four views (plan, profile, back three-quarter, front three-quarter); her in front three-quarter, walking and standing; the younger faces for the pictures. Expressions as layered flat shapes with a small library of eye, brow and mouth states.
- **Shots: about 12 days.** Roughly 70 to 90 shots of mostly still frames with small, precise motion, including the bus crowd, which is the most expensive shot in the look.
- **Light and grade passes: about 3 days.**
- **Total.** About 6 weeks for one artist-engineer, not counting voices, sound and the mix. The main risk is faces in motion at medium size, which the stillness of the look keeps to a minimum.
